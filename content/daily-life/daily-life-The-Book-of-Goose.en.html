These are the three passages from The Book of Goose that resonated with me the most.

The first passage immediately reminded me of my own sense of confusion in life. So often, we hold grand ambitions for ourselves, yet as we slowly realize how much effort it truly takes to reach them—or that we may never reach them at all within the limits of a single lifetime—what remains is a kind of endless, directionless pain. In response, we turn to faith for hope. But deep down, I also know that what I truly need may not be hope, but learning how to let go.

Through the author's description of happiness, I came to a realization. I used to feel that happiness was often described through an accumulation of words, focusing only on the fleeting moment when it occurs. Here, however, happiness is revealed through its consequences. By tracing happiness backward from its results, the idea becomes far more convincing. We often find ourselves reminiscing about the past because it was beautiful—but perhaps it is also because the present feels unsatisfactory. Many of the pains we endure are, in a way, attempts to persuade ourselves that a better future is still waiting.

I also deeply appreciate the final passage. I have traveled to many places, and in this age of information overload, the internet is filled with images of beauty from all over the world. Yet when I think back, my original impulse to travel began in middle school geography class. As teachers introduced different places through images and stories, I remember thinking to myself that I had to see these places with my own eyes someday.

In the end, the book reflects on the friendship between Fabien and Agnes. It becomes clear that Fabien created an imagined "older brother" to say the things he himself could not say to Agnes. As Fabien suggests, perhaps there are many things Agnes never truly understood—and that lack of understanding itself makes certain outcomes impossible.
