It always reminds me of a mud swamp where one is caught between two difficulties, where each step almost sinks the entire shoe into the heavy, oppressive mud.

And I move my steps, gasping for breath in this mud swamp. Nothing ahead, no one behind. Only the dim mud swamp extending endlessly.

It seems time flows with great difficulty because of my pace. Those around me have long since moved far ahead. Only I and my time crawl painfully through the mud.

Lift the right foot, then raise the left foot. I cannot judge where I am, nor do I have confidence that I am moving in the right direction. The reason I move step by step is because I must move, regardless of where I'm going.

I have reached the age of 20, springs and autumns have passed, yet my life remains unchanged.

Norwegian Wood

Death is not the opposite of life, but exists eternally as part of life.

Death itself has no meaning; only the living can bestow meaning upon it.

But emotions are ultimately not knowledge. We cannot seal them away in drawer after drawer and act as if nothing happened.

Countless premature deaths of youth, the disappearance of precious things—we can only accept the unfinished regrets, feel unexpected sorrow, and continue, persistently, moving forward, wandering everywhere.

Naoko's greatest symptom is the loss of expression, the inability to speak, because the dead no longer need to establish new relationships with the world.

Watanabe is the chain that connects them to the world, but the chain breaks inch by inch.

A star explodes and collapses into a black hole, infinite gravity devouring all life. No matter how hard Watanabe pulls Naoko's hand, she ultimately turns to ashes.

Life as a cookie jar.

The cookie jar contains all kinds of cookies, both liked and disliked. If you first eat all the ones you like, then all that remains are the ones you don't like much.

When encountering trouble, I think this way:

Deal with this first. Life is a cookie jar.

Norwegian Wood

After reading the entire book, what moved me most was not the plot itself, but Murakami's ability to express those subtle feelings we often forget but leave a lasting impression.

That feeling of "something falling out of my brain" is not an emotional outburst, but a "real state." Like the cookie jar metaphor, it connects to shame, but also to utility.

Sometimes, darkness presses down on us so heavily that we can't breathe. We choose to "close our eyes" and let time pass. But this is not escape, it's a way of survival.

In the end, I realize that life and death are not opposites, but intertwined. This understanding makes me more clearly aware of my own existence, and those inexpressible yet real emotions.
