"Life is like a biscuit tin.
The biscuit tin contains all sorts of biscuits,
some you like and some you don't.
If you eagerly eat all the ones you like first,
then all that's left are the ones you don't like much.
When you encounter trouble, think this way:
just get through this,
life is just a biscuit tin."

I was deeply struck by how clearly the author articulated those fleeting feelings. I recall the phrase "a string snapped in my brain" from the book—a quiet, profound break where a part of one's ability to experience life suddenly loses its sound, a state so real it's hard to ignore.

Norwegian Wood

The mention of a "candy box" in the book reminds me of the idea that "avoidance, though shameful, can be useful." I reflect that often, people aren't brave but simply too tired, choosing to close their eyes and let time pass through difficult days.

These reflections also lead me to ponder the nature of life and death—they are not opposites but interwoven shadows and light in daily life. Sometimes, living itself carries a sense of weightlessness, yet it is precisely in this state that one realizes they are still breathing and feeling.
