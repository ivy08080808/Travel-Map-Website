My Favorite Quotes

"No matter how much time passes, those we have loved never slip away from us entirely."

No doubt there have been moments when your life has taken a bit of a leap forward; and no doubt you look back upon those moments with self-assurance and pride. But was there really no third party deserving of even a modicum of credit? Some mentor, family friend, or schoolmate who gave timely advice, made a introduction, or put in a complimentary word?

It is a sad but unavoidable fact of life, that as we age our social circles grow smaller. Whether from increased habit or diminished vigor, we suddenly find ourselves in the company of just a few familiar faces. So I view it as an incredible stroke of good fortune at this stage in my life to have found such a fine new friend.

What impressed me most about "A Gentleman in Moscow" was not the dramatic plot, but how the book consistently addresses a single question: When a person cannot change their environment, what can they still control?

The protagonist, Count Alexander Rostov, is confined to a hotel for life, with severely restricted space and actions. From an external perspective, his life seems like a failure.

However, the book's true message is not about "how cruel fate is," but about "attitude" and "how to redefine adversity itself."

The Count neither denies his predicament nor pretends everything is fine. Instead, he chooses to approach life in his own way: maintaining etiquette, taste, respect for others, and not abandoning his sensitivity to details.

This attitude transforms the hotel, which was like a prison, into a place where he observes the world, builds relationships, and finds new meaning.

This made me realize that adversity itself does not automatically confer meaning or suffering; what truly matters is how we perceive it.

"A Gentleman in Moscow" does not promote blind optimism but offers a quieter, yet more powerful belief: When we cannot choose our circumstances, we can still choose our stance. And that stance will determine whether we still live as ourselves.

The book made me rethink "freedom," suggesting it's not just about unrestricted action but an inner ability to always maintain a "stance" or "attitude" regardless of the situation.

After reading this book, I have a wish in my heart: one day I will visit the Metropolitan Hotel, order the same afternoon tea and dinner as the Count, and re-savor the book.
